Summary

Ph.D. researcher in complex systems, working across evolutionary biology, network science, AI, social science and energy. My main work, in bio-inspired computation and collective intelligence, asks how evolution shapes systems that must decide amid real-world randomness. Evolution relies on logical redundancy, where a system's decisions rest on only some of its inputs, to produce better collective decisions. I study this with Boolean networks, cellular automata and agent-based models in the CASCI lab at Binghamton University. At the Católica Biomedical Research Centre in Lisbon, I build open-source Python tools that explain the dynamics of biomedical networks, from gene regulation and comorbidity to epidemic transmission and brain networks. I also study the social biases of large language model (LLM) agents: in a social segregation model, the social context an agent is given shapes segregation patterns that mirror real-world data such as the U.S. census, with large implications for the AI agents now entering online spaces. And I model energy systems, from the spread of solar, wind and battery moratoria across New York towns to optimizing the state's power grid with a capacity-expansion model. I came to research after fifteen years writing and producing for film, television and advertising, and I bring that storytelling to communicating complex, interdisciplinary ideas effectively. I'm partial to fantasy and science fiction.

Collaborative Decisions group (Dr. Neha Patankar) · Binghamton University, State University of New York · Binghamton, NY, USA
  • Analyzing town and county moratoria on large solar, wind and battery projects across New York State, so that GenX, a capacity-expansion model that finds the lowest-cost mix of power plants, storage and transmission lines, can account for them.
  • Modeling why and when towns adopt a moratorium, and whether one town's decision spreads to its neighbors, with statistical models that isolate the main factors behind local decisions; a neighboring town's adoption raises a town's monthly odds of adopting by about 11%.

Research projects

LLM Agents in the Sakoda-Schelling Segregation Model
Jan 2025 – present
  • Modeling residential choice with LLM agents in the Sakoda-Schelling segregation model: each agent is given a social identity (racial, ethnic, income or political) and decides whether to move or stay, and the resulting segregation is compared with U.S. census data. The models show biases across social contexts, evidence of the predispositions AI agents carry, with large implications as they begin to populate the online world.
  • Built the full simulation framework: the simulation protocol and stopping rules, the LLM agents and their prompts, an interface that makes agents answer in a fixed format so their replies can be read reliably, and the method that reads each agent's move-or-stay probability directly from the model.
